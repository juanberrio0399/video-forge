## CHAPTER 1 — Scaling the number  (0:55-2:15)
NARRATION: "Let's start with the big one. In 2025, YouTube brought in more than sixty billion dollars in a single year. Sixty billion. That number's too big to feel, so let's shrink it down. Sixty billion a year is about a hundred and sixty-four million dollars a day. Every day. That means in one ordinary Tuesday, YouTube makes more money than most successful companies make in an entire year. Zoom in further. That's roughly six point eight million dollars an hour. A hundred and fourteen thousand dollars a minute — more than most people earn in a year, gone in sixty seconds. And down at the smallest slice: about nineteen hundred dollars a second. Blink, and there goes a used car. And here's the thing to hold onto — that clock never stops. Three in the morning, holidays, right now while you're watching this. It just keeps counting."

## CHAPTER 2 — Where it comes from: the ads  (2:15-3:45)
NARRATION: "So who's actually paying? The first and biggest bucket is ads — the clips that play before and during almost every video. In 2024 alone, YouTube ads pulled in about thirty-six billion dollars. And the end of that year was a monster: in the final three months, ad revenue broke ten billion dollars in a single quarter for the very first time. Part of the reason? The US election. Political campaigns flooded YouTube with ad money, because that's where the attention is now. But think about what's really happening here. YouTube doesn't make those videos. You do. Creators do. YouTube built the stage, and then sells tickets to the show in the form of your attention. Every time you wait five seconds to skip an ad — that's the product being sold. And it works, thirty-six billion dollars a year worth of it."
[VIS: barra de ingresos por ads 2024, con el Q4 >$10B resaltado; icono de "attention -> money"]

## CHAPTER 3 — The part nobody notices: subscriptions  (3:45-5:15)
NARRATION: "But ads are only half the story. Here's the bucket most people forget YouTube even has: subscriptions. There's YouTube Premium, where you pay every month to kill the ads. There's YouTube Music. And then there's the quiet giant — YouTube TV. Yeah, YouTube sells cable. Real live television — sports, news, local channels — streamed over the internet. And it is not small. By late 2025, YouTube TV passed ten million subscribers, each paying about eighty-three dollars a month. Do that math: ten million people times eighty-three dollars is over eight hundred million dollars — a month. That one product alone is pulling in roughly ten billion dollars a year. The company everyone thinks of as 'free videos' quietly became one of the biggest TV providers in America. Quick question before we go on — did you even know YouTube sold cable? Drop a 'no' in the comments, I'm genuinely curious how many people missed this one."

## CHAPTER 4 — Bigger than Netflix  (5:15-6:20)
NARRATION: "Now put the two buckets together — ads plus subscriptions — and you cross that sixty-billion-dollar line. And that number is big enough to do something that felt impossible a few years ago. In 2025, YouTube quietly passed Netflix. Let that sink in. Netflix spends billions producing polished shows and movies, with studios and actors and scripts. YouTube produces almost none of its own content — it's cat videos, tutorials, and people filming in their bedrooms. And that platform now brings in more total money than Hollywood's favorite streaming empire. The amateurs out-earned the professionals. And it happened without YouTube picking up a single camera."

## CHAPTER 6 — The twist: the paycheck engine  (6:50-8:10)
NARRATION: "For every dollar a video earns in ads, YouTube keeps forty-five cents — and sends the other fifty-five cents straight to the creator who made it. Fifty-five percent. That's not a tip; that's the majority of the money leaving Google and landing in the hands of regular people. And the scale of it is staggering. YouTube has said that over the last four years, it paid out more than one hundred billion dollars to creators, artists, and media companies. A hundred billion dollars — to people, many of whom started with nothing but a phone and an idea. So YouTube isn't just a money machine. It's the biggest paycheck engine the internet has ever built for ordinary people. Every second that counter ticks up, part of it is turning into someone's rent, someone's new camera, someone's full-time job that didn't exist ten years ago."
